\begin{abstract}
Occlusion makes crowd navigation a problem of controlling under missing information:
a pedestrian that disappears from the sensor may still occupy the robot's intended path.
Retaining a predicted position addresses this loss only partially, because the reliability
of that prediction changes during an observation gap.
This paper presents \method{}, a training-free navigation framework that combines
recursive Bernoulli--Gaussian pedestrian beliefs with continuous trajectory optimization.
Visible detections update track states, while missed detections propagate position
uncertainty and modify existence confidence according to visibility evidence.
The planner evaluates probability mass within robot--pedestrian collision regions and
uses route-separated sampling to search for executable, goal-directed motion.
The formulation consumes the posterior directly in trajectory evaluation rather than
adding uncertainty features to a learned policy.
In a completed six-scene CrowdNav study with 600 paired episodes per arm,
the full-posterior method achieves 97.17\% success and 2.00\% collision,
compared with 93.00\% and 7.00\% for constant-velocity memory under the same planner.
The reduction corresponds to 42 versus 12 collisions, with a 0.129\,s increase in
failure-penalized task time.
Controlled ablations attribute the clearest benefit to position uncertainty;
the additional contribution of existence weighting is not statistically resolved.
A calibrated duration-dependent geometric margin incurs substantially more timeouts,
whereas fairly calibrated EWMA and conformal alternatives remain competitive under
severe observation noise.
These results support probabilistic treatment of occluded tracks without establishing
universal Bayesian superiority or a closed-loop safety guarantee.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{sec:introduction}
Autonomous robots operating among pedestrians must reconcile collision avoidance,
timely arrival, and predictable motion. These objectives become more difficult when
the robot cannot continuously observe everyone nearby. One pedestrian can hide another,
a previously visible person can disappear behind an obstacle, and an apparently open
route can become occupied before the next reliable observation. A navigation algorithm
must therefore reason not only about where a pedestrian is predicted to move, but also
about how much confidence should be placed in that prediction.

Reactive geometric methods and learned crowd-navigation policies address different
parts of this problem. Reciprocal collision avoidance constructs locally admissible
velocities using a model of how other agents respond. Learned policies and
value-based methods encode interaction patterns from experience, using pairwise
representations, attention, or recurrent state.
These approaches provide important baselines, but a missing observation remains a
substantive modeling decision: the controller can discard the pedestrian, extrapolate
a remembered position, or maintain a distribution over the pedestrian's possible state.
The last option is useful only if the planner converts that distribution into better
closed-loop decisions.

This distinction motivates the present work. A more accurate predictive density is
not by itself a navigation contribution. A controller may react identically to two
different uncertainty estimates, a conservative risk region may prevent useful
progress, or a restricted trajectory family may contain no suitable evasive motion.
Consequently, prediction quality, uncertainty representation, and control generation
must be evaluated together. The relevant question is not whether a Bayesian model
can be attached to a navigation system, but whether its posterior provides information
that a controller can use beyond a deterministic memory or a calibrated safety margin.

We study this question through a training-free architecture. A recursive
Bernoulli--Gaussian filter maintains one belief for each associated pedestrian.
The posterior contains an existence probability, a mean position and velocity, and
a covariance. During occlusion, the track continues to evolve rather than disappearing
from the planner's input. A continuous model predictive controller then evaluates
the probability mass inside candidate collision regions and searches several
motion families separately. It applies the first control of an evaluated trajectory,
rather than averaging distinct evasive maneuvers into a new, unevaluated motion.
The framework requires neither imitation learning nor reinforcement learning,
and does not use a learned navigation policy.

The Bayesian component is a state estimator and the uncertainty source for planning,
not a claim of mathematical exclusivity. Other probabilistic estimators and calibrated
prediction sets can represent uncertainty. Moreover, in the clean observation model
used by part of our benchmark, the covariance after an exact detection is determined
by the subsequent time without observations. This makes a duration-dependent geometric
margin an especially important alternative. A meaningful evaluation must give such
simple alternatives enough development data to operate competitively.

The completed experiments support a specific, narrower conclusion than universal
dominance. Under occlusion, replacing constant-velocity memory with the full posterior
reduces collisions from 42 to 12 in 600 paired episodes. Using the same Bayesian
track means without covariance or existence weighting produces 39 collisions.
However, forcing existence weights to one while retaining covariance produces
16 collisions, a difference from the full method that is not statistically resolved.
In addition, under fairly calibrated severe noise, the full method does not establish
a significant success- or collision-rate advantage over EWMA and conformal alternatives.
These findings distinguish useful posterior information from an unnecessarily broad
claim that all of its components are indispensable.

The main contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A fully specified occlusion-belief-to-control formulation integrates recursive
  pedestrian state estimation, collision-region probability evaluation, and
  route-separated continuous trajectory optimization. Its assumptions and fallback
  behavior are stated explicitly.
  \item Controlled closed-loop experiments separate current sensing, deterministic
  memory, posterior means, position covariance, and existence weighting, identifying
  which parts of the representation account for the observed navigation improvement.
  \item Calibration-matched alternatives, a duration-margin control, and single-process
  timing measurements characterize the method's practical benefits and limitations.
\end{enumerate}
The formulation builds on established filtering, collision-probability, and
sampling-based optimization tools. The study focuses on their integration and
the closed-loop contribution of posterior information under occlusion.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related approaches, and
Sections~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:results} present the method and evaluation.

\section{RELATED WORK}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Reactive and Learned Crowd Navigation}
ORCA distributes collision-avoidance responsibility between interacting agents and
constructs velocity constraints from their relative geometry. Its
reciprocity assumption differs from the non-responsive-human setting considered here:
when pedestrians do not account for the robot, the robot must compensate for that
asymmetry. This does not make reciprocal methods invalid baselines, but the interaction
assumption must be disclosed.

CADRL and related deep value-learning methods evaluate the consequences of candidate
actions using learned interaction values. Recurrent representations
support variable numbers of surrounding agents, while SARL aggregates
human--human and human--robot interactions through attention. DSRNN
structures recurrent computation around interaction relations.
Prediction-aware interaction graphs further connect anticipated pedestrian motion to
navigation. These methods motivate the retained-checkpoint benchmark
in this paper, but that benchmark does not isolate the effect of Bayesian inference:
both the state representation and the controller differ from \method{}.

Our principal attribution experiments therefore keep the MPC fixed and intervene on
the information supplied to it. The comparison with older learned methods is useful
as a navigation reference, whereas the controlled interventions address why posterior
uncertainty matters within the proposed architecture.

\subsection{Occlusion, Belief, and Object Persistence}
People-as-Sensors learns an occlusion-aware representation from observable crowd
behavior and incorporates it into navigation. Belief-aided navigation
using Bayesian reinforcement learning also addresses humans in blind
spots. These approaches show that using belief in partially observed
crowds is not itself a new research direction. Our implementation instead maintains
explicit parametric tracks and consumes their posterior in a non-learned MPC.
Its strongest existing evidence concerns pedestrians that were observed and
subsequently occluded. It does not establish recovery of never-observed pedestrians
from visible-agent behavior.

The Bayesian filtering machinery follows established recursive state-estimation
principles. A missed measurement is not equivalent to evidence of
absence: an occluded track should generally be propagated, while a track predicted
inside a reliably observed empty region can receive negative detection evidence.
Existence probabilities have also been considered in motion planning for automated
driving. Consequently, neither object persistence nor multiplying
collision risk by existence confidence can be claimed as unprecedented in isolation.

\subsection{Modern Probabilistic and Interactive MPC}
Topology-driven parallel MPC searches distinct trajectory classes to reduce the
limitations of a single local optimization; T-MPC++ combines guided candidates with
an additional unguided planner. This is a particularly relevant comparator
because our route-separated search also attempts to preserve alternative passing
motions. Our finite collection of seeded velocity populations is not equivalent to
a certified enumeration of homotopy classes.

Scenario-based SH-MPC addresses the probability of collision over a trajectory
through scenario optimization. Its risk object differs from a
per-time-step probability computed from independent marginal pedestrian beliefs.
Giving the two planners numerically identical risk parameters would therefore not
establish equal conservatism or equal guarantees. We compare operating points in terms
of measured safety and efficiency, and retain the distinction between their formal
assumptions.

SICNav embeds an interaction model in a bilevel planning problem,
and SICNav-Diffusion combines interactive navigation with diffusion trajectory
predictions. Their interaction assumptions and optimization
structures differ from independent constant-velocity belief propagation.
Occlusion-aware consistent MPC explicitly considers occlusion-related planning
structure. This literature prevents us from describing passive
uncertainty propagation as active perception or contingent belief-space planning.
The current method neither optimizes expected information gain nor branches its
future controls on hypothetical observations.

\subsection{Calibration and Sampling-Based Optimization}
Bayesian filtering is sensitive to its observation and process models. A posterior
can be internally consistent yet miscalibrated when pedestrians turn unexpectedly,
identities are incorrect, or sensor noise differs from the assumed distribution.
Conformal prediction provides an alternative route to calibrated sets under its
own assumptions. It is not intrinsically restricted to one global
radius: nonconformity scores, conditioning variables, and adaptation mechanisms can
produce different widths in different contexts. Our calibrated conformal and EWMA
controls should thus be read as particular implementations, not exhaustive
representatives of those families.

Cross-entropy trajectory optimization and sampling reuse have a substantial
literature, including sample-efficient real-time planning.
The proposed controller uses this established optimization family. The method-level
question is how persistent uncertain tracks, probability-mass risk, and separate
motion populations work together in partially observed navigation. The completed
ablations support uncertainty-aware planning; a claim of a novel optimization
principle would require additional evidence and is not made here.

\section{METHODOLOGY}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Problem Formulation and System Overview}
The robot has planar position $p^r_t\in\R^2$, velocity $v^r_t\in\R^2$, radius
$r_r$, and goal $g$. Pedestrian $i$ has state
$x^i_t=[(p^i_t)^\top,(v^i_t)^\top]^\top\in\R^4$ and radius $r_i$.
Only a subset of pedestrians is detected at time $t$. Let $Z_t$ be the associated
detection set and $\mathcal V_t$ the robot's visibility information. Navigation seeks
an executable trajectory that reaches $g$ while controlling collision exposure and
unnecessary delay.

Two visibility notions must be separated. Sensor occlusion determines which
pedestrians the robot observes. The environment parameter
\texttt{robot.visible=false} instead determines whether pedestrians account for the
robot when generating their own motion. The latter is fixed to false in the completed
benchmark. It does not imply that the robot itself receives no pedestrian observations.

The deployed information interface contains detections, observed velocities, radii,
and visibility evidence, but not hidden ground-truth positions, pedestrian goals, or
the true number of unobserved people. Ground truth is reserved for environment
evolution and outcome auditing. The current simulator provides track identity
association; handling identity switches is outside the validated scope.

\subsection{Recursive Pedestrian Beliefs}
\subsubsection{State and Existence Representation}
Each maintained track is represented by
\begin{equation}
b^i_t=\{r^i_t,\mu^i_t,P^i_t,a^i_t\},
\end{equation}
where $r^i_t$ is the probability of existence, $\mu^i_t$ and $P^i_t$ are the
conditional Gaussian state mean and covariance, and $a^i_t$ counts missed observation
steps. The Bernoulli track has empty-set probability $1-r^i_t$ and singleton density
$r^i_t\mathcal N(x;\mu^i_t,P^i_t)$. The collection is factorized across associated
tracks. It is not a full posterior over correlated crowd trajectories or a
multi-hypothesis data-association distribution.

The formal configuration creates tracks from visible detections. It does not enable
a general spatial birth prior for never-observed people. Existence uncertainty
therefore concerns the persistence and removal of previously observed tracks.
This distinction is essential when interpreting the term \emph{occlusion awareness}.

\subsubsection{Prediction}
For sampling interval $\Delta t$, the constant-velocity model is
\begin{equation}
F=
\begin{bmatrix}I_2&\Delta t I_2\\0&I_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad
Q=\sigma_a^2
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\Delta t^4}{4}I_2&\frac{\Delta t^3}{2}I_2\\
\frac{\Delta t^3}{2}I_2&\Delta t^2 I_2
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:process}
\end{equation}
The predictive update is
\begin{align}
\mu^{i,-}_t&=F\mu^{i,+}_{t-1},&
P^{i,-}_t&=FP^{i,+}_{t-1}F^\top+Q,\\
r^{i,-}_t&=p_Sr^{i,+}_{t-1},
\end{align}
with survival parameter $p_S$. This model captures the growth of extrapolation
uncertainty; it does not infer a hidden destination or assume that a pedestrian
will negotiate with the robot. Unexpected turning or acceleration is represented
only through the process-noise approximation.

\subsubsection{Detection and Missed-Detection Updates}
For an associated detection $z_t^i$ containing position and velocity, the measurement
matrix is $I_4$ and the noise covariance is
\begin{equation}
R=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_p^2,\sigma_p^2,\sigma_v^2,\sigma_v^2).
\end{equation}
The Gaussian update uses
\begin{align}
K_t&=P_t^-(P_t^-+R)^{-1},\\
\mu_t^+&=\mu_t^-+K_t(z_t-\mu_t^-),\\
P_t^+&=(I-K_t)P_t^-(I-K_t)^\top+K_tRK_t^\top.
\label{eq:joseph}
\end{align}
The Joseph form maintains the covariance structure in finite precision.
In the exact-observation condition, the implementation explicitly sets the state
to the measurement and the covariance to zero. A confirmed detection resets the
missed-step counter and sets existence confidence close to one.

When no associated detection is available, the predicted mean is queried against
the visibility map. If this location should be observable but no detection occurs,
negative evidence gives
\begin{equation}
r_t^+=\frac{(1-p_D)r_t^-}{1-p_Dr_t^-}.
\label{eq:miss}
\end{equation}
If the predicted location is occluded or unobserved, this negative-evidence update
is not applied. The update is a mean-location approximation: it does not integrate
detection probability over every position in the Gaussian posterior.
Tracks are removed after low existence confidence or a maximum missed age.
These management thresholds are part of the model, not measurements of the true
number of remaining pedestrians.

\subsubsection{Prediction Across the Planning Horizon}
For future step $k$, the planner receives
\begin{align}
\mu_{t,k}^i&=F^k\mu_t^i,\\
P_{t,k}^i&=F^kP_t^i(F^k)^\top+
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}F^jQ(F^j)^\top.
\label{eq:horizon}
\end{align}
The implementation uses the current existence weight over the candidate horizon;
it does not repeatedly multiply this weight by $p_S$ for every hypothetical step.
The position mean and covariance are the corresponding upper blocks of
\eqref{eq:horizon}.

After an exact observation, $P=0$. With $m$ missed steps, the future covariance
satisfies
\begin{equation}
P_{m+k}=\sum_{j=0}^{m+k-1}F^jQ(F^j)^\top.
\label{eq:agecov}
\end{equation}
Under these assumptions, covariance is a function of observation age and prediction
horizon, not a learned, person-specific measure of behavioral surprise.
Equation~\eqref{eq:agecov} directly motivates the age-dependent margin baseline.
Replacing this equation by an identical lookup table would be an implementation
optimization, not a genuinely different non-Bayesian method.

\subsection{Collision-Region Probability}
\subsubsection{Probability for One Pedestrian}
Consider robot candidate position $p^r_{t,k}$ and pedestrian position distribution
$\mathcal N(\mu^p_{i,k},\Sigma_{i,k})$. Define the interaction radius
\begin{equation}
R_i=r_r+r_i+m_h,
\end{equation}
where $m_h$ is a geometric buffer shared across the matched planning interventions.
The desired conditional collision probability is the Gaussian mass inside a disc:
\begin{equation}
q_{i,k}=
\int_{\norm{y-p^r_{t,k}}\le R_i}
\mathcal N(y;\mu^p_{i,k},\Sigma_{i,k})\,dy.
\label{eq:disc}
\end{equation}
For isotropic position covariance $\Sigma_{i,k}=s_{i,k}^2 I_2$, let
$d_{i,k}=\norm{\mu^p_{i,k}-p^r_{t,k}}$. The integral becomes
\begin{equation}
q_{i,k}=F_{\chi'^2_2(\lambda_{i,k})}
\left(\frac{R_i^2}{s_{i,k}^2}\right),\qquad
\lambda_{i,k}=\frac{d_{i,k}^2}{s_{i,k}^2},
\label{eq:ncx}
\end{equation}
where $F_{\chi'^2_2(\lambda)}$ denotes a noncentral chi-square CDF.
As variance approaches zero, the implementation uses the corresponding deterministic
overlap indicator.

The present process and measurement models produce isotropic position uncertainty
under the evaluated initialization. If an arbitrary anisotropic covariance is
supplied, the implementation replaces it by
$s^2=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)/2$; \eqref{eq:ncx} is then an isotropic approximation,
not an exact anisotropic integral. General anisotropic collision integration is
not claimed by this version.

Existence-weighted probability is
\begin{equation}
\pi_{i,k}=r_t^i q_{i,k}.
\end{equation}
The effect of this weight is tested separately from covariance. It is possible for
track management to use existence even when the planning weight is fixed to one;
the two interventions should not be conflated.

\subsubsection{Combining Pedestrians and Time}
Under the factorized pedestrian approximation,
\begin{equation}
p_k=1-\prod_i(1-\pi_{i,k}),\qquad
\ell_k=-\sum_i\log(1-\pi_{i,k}).
\label{eq:hazard}
\end{equation}
The negative-log-survival quantity $\ell_k$ permits stable accumulation and yields
$p_k=1-\exp(-\ell_k)$. Numerical clipping prevents logarithmic singularities.
The factorization ignores correlated pedestrian motion and is therefore a modeling
assumption rather than an unconditional safety certificate.

The risk limit varies over the horizon:
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_k=\epsilon_{\mathrm{near}}+
(\epsilon_{\mathrm{far}}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{near}})
\left(\frac{k-1}{H-1}\right)^\gamma,\quad k=1,\ldots,H.
\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
The evaluated baseline uses $\gamma=2$.
Each step is checked against its own limit. Neither summing $\ell_k$ in the objective
nor enforcing \eqref{eq:budget} computes the probability of any collision over the
whole trajectory: successive pedestrian predictions are temporally correlated.
In particular, these limits are not interchangeable with the horizon-level risk
guarantee of SH-MPC.

\subsection{Continuous Trajectory Construction}
\subsubsection{Executable Motion and Swept Geometry}
In the completed holonomic benchmark, controls are planar velocities
$U=(u_0,\ldots,u_{H-1})$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
\norm{u_k}\le v_{\max},\qquad
\norm{u_k-u_{k-1}}\le a_{\max}\Delta t.
\end{equation}
Candidate samples are sequentially projected onto these limits, then propagated by
\begin{equation}
p^r_{k+1}=p^r_k+\Delta t\,u_k.
\end{equation}
The projection is included before trajectory scoring. The selected command is
therefore scored in the same holonomic representation that is executed.
The differential-drive comparison is a separate protocol because this equivalence
does not automatically survive a conversion to angular and linear velocities.

Endpoint risk alone can miss a crossing between two simulation steps. The planner
also computes a swept geometric clearance against the predicted pedestrian means.
For relative endpoints $d_0,d_1$, define $\delta=d_1-d_0$ and
\begin{equation}
\alpha^\star=\operatorname{clip}
\left(-\frac{d_0^\top\delta}{\norm{\delta}^2},0,1\right),\qquad
c=\norm{d_0+\alpha^\star\delta}-R_i.
\label{eq:swept}
\end{equation}
Degenerate zero relative displacement uses $\alpha^\star=0$.
This check is geometric: it does not turn the endpoint probability model into a
continuous-time stochastic collision guarantee. An independent swept-overlap audit
is retained during evaluation.

\subsubsection{Goal-Directed Objective}
For a candidate trajectory, define distance to the goal region as
$d_g(p)=\max(\norm{p-g}-r_r,0)$. The objective combines goal progress, control effort,
smoothness, collision-related penalties, and failure to make progress:
\begin{align}
J(U)={}&w_g\sum_{k=1}^{H_a}d_g(p^r_k)
+w_T d_g(p^r_{H_a})
+w_u\sum_{k=0}^{H_a-1}\norm{u_k}^2 \nonumber\\
&+w_\Delta\sum_{k=0}^{H_a-1}\norm{u_k-u_{k-1}}^2
+w_{\mathrm{prob}}\sum_{k=1}^{H_a}\ell_k \nonumber\\
&+J_{\mathrm{geom}}(U)+J_{\mathrm{risk}}(U)+J_{\mathrm{progress}}(U).
\label{eq:objective}
\end{align}
Here $H_a$ denotes the active portion of the horizon up to goal entry, if any.
The goal-entry step remains subject to collision checks; steps after termination
do not incur fictitious post-arrival interaction cost.
$J_{\mathrm{geom}}$ penalizes overlap and insufficient clearance,
$J_{\mathrm{risk}}$ penalizes excess over the scheduled probability limits, and
$J_{\mathrm{progress}}$ discourages negligible progress.
Their role is to shape candidate ranking; explicit feasibility labels are also
used rather than relying solely on a finite weighted penalty.

\subsubsection{Route-Separated Sampling}
A single Gaussian over all useful controls can merge incompatible passing motions.
For example, averaging left- and right-passing trajectories may produce a straight
path through a pedestrian. To avoid executing that average, we maintain separate
sampling populations initialized from the shifted previous solution, left-biased
motion, right-biased motion, and waiting-oriented motion.
These are motion seeds, not a complete topological classification.

At each search iteration, temporally correlated perturbations are added to the
population means. After projection and evaluation, elites are selected within
each population. The sampling mean is smoothed toward its elites and each
standard deviation is bounded below to retain exploration. The implementation uses
short-range correlated perturbations; no claim of a learned diffusion model or
long-horizon autoregressive noise process is made.

The best evaluated control sequence is retained across iterations and populations.
The robot executes its first command. Distribution means are search parameters,
not actions to be averaged across distinct successful routes.
This design follows the broader sampling-MPC and cross-entropy family;
its incremental value relative to modern multi-route optimization remains a
question for the independent comparator study.

\subsubsection{Feasibility Hierarchy and Fallback}
Candidates are prioritized in three groups. First, a trajectory that satisfies
the geometric and probabilistic checks over the active horizon is preferred.
Second, when none does, trajectories satisfying the first-step checks are ranked
by horizon slack and objective value. Third, if no first-step-feasible candidate
has been found, the controller prioritizes immediate nominal geometric clearance
before applying the remaining ranking criteria.

The third group can violate the nominal risk constraints. It is a practical
best-available-motion fallback, not a safety-preserving invariant set.
Consequently, this algorithm should be described as risk-aware MPC, not as a
recursively feasible or provably collision-free controller.
Fallback frequency, feasibility class, and subsequent outcomes belong in the final
failure analysis.

\begin{algorithm}[tb]
\caption{Recursive belief and route-separated receding-horizon control}
\label{alg:main}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Prior tracks, previous solution, detections $Z_t$, visibility $\mathcal V_t$
\State Predict each track using $F,Q,p_S$
\State Update detected tracks; apply justified missed-detection evidence
\State Remove expired tracks and initialize newly detected tracks
\State Predict surviving track means and covariances for $H$ steps
\State Initialize separate warm-start, left, right, and waiting populations
\For{$j=1,\ldots,K$}
  \State Sample velocity sequences and project onto the robot limits
  \State Compute rollouts, swept clearance, collision mass, and objective
  \State Assign feasibility classes and update each population's elites
  \State Retain the best evaluated sequence across all populations
\EndFor
\State Execute its first control and shift the sequence for the next cycle
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Computational Structure and Scope}
With $B$ candidates, $K$ iterations, $H$ prediction steps, and $M$ maintained
pedestrians, the dominant pairwise evaluation scales as $O(KBHM)$.
The implementation vectorizes this calculation, but the completed measurements
use a desktop CPU path. The presence of a GPU in the host machine does not imply
that the planner uses GPU kernels.
There is no policy checkpoint to deploy; the executable artifact consists of
the filtering and planning implementation, its numerical dependencies, and a
frozen parameter configuration.

This formulation makes uncertainty operational: changing covariance changes
collision-region probability and can change the selected trajectory. It does not
make Bayesian inference logically indispensable. A calibrated set estimator or
another predictive distribution can provide relevant information as well.
The experiments test whether the particular recursive posterior is useful and
which simpler alternatives remain competitive, rather than attempting to prove
that only a Bayesian algorithm could solve the navigation task.

\section{EXPERIMENTS}
\label{sec:experiments}
\subsection{Research Questions}
The evaluation distinguishes three questions. First, does retaining occluded pedestrians
improve navigation relative to current sensing alone? Second, does position uncertainty
provide useful information beyond a remembered or filtered mean? Third, can a simpler,
fairly calibrated uncertainty representation achieve the same safety--efficiency
tradeoff?

These questions require different comparisons. Older learned baselines test the
complete navigation system; they cannot determine whether the gain comes from
filtering or trajectory search. Posterior-mean and existence interventions address
specific components. Calibration-matched alternatives test the necessity of the
particular uncertainty treatment.

\subsection{CrowdNav Benchmark}
\subsubsection{Scenarios and Physical Settings}
The completed holonomic evaluation uses six configurations drawn from circle and
square crossing geometries, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}. The six
configurations vary crowd size and spatial extent; they are not six unrelated
environment types. No corridor or merging experiment is implied by this table.

The control interval is 0.25\,s, the time limit is 25\,s, and the holonomic robot
is limited to 1.0\,m/s with acceleration bounded by 2.0\,m/s$^2$.
The benchmark robot and pedestrian radii are 0.30\,m, and the goal tolerance is
the same 0.30\,m: the simulator declares arrival when the robot centre enters a
disc of one robot radius about the goal. The \texttt{success\_radius} entry of
0.25\,m present in the environment configuration file is not read by the active
environment and does not affect any reported outcome. The local visibility grid
has 0.25\,m resolution and spans approximately 5\,m in each direction from
the robot.

Pedestrian response to the robot is disabled
(\texttt{robot.visible=false}). This is a deliberately demanding protocol, and it
has a consequence that matters when comparing controllers: because pedestrians do
not yield, holding still is not a safe action. A controller whose fallback on an
infeasible or low-confidence step is to brake becomes a stationary obstacle that
walking pedestrians can reach. Completion and collision rates under this protocol
therefore penalise conservative fallback behaviour more sharply than an
environment with reactive pedestrians would, and this should be kept in view when
the numbers are compared with published results obtained under reactive crowds.
The current-sensor condition supplies only visible detections, whereas remembered
tracks can persist through subsequent observation gaps in the other conditions.

Figure~\ref{fig:observability} shows what this protocol removes from the robot's
input. Two mechanisms are involved and they are not interchangeable. A pedestrian
can be hidden behind another body, which is inter-agent occlusion proper, or can
simply be farther away than the sensor reaches. Because these scenes contain no
walls or static obstacles, every occluder is another pedestrian, so genuine
occlusion requires the crowd to be dense enough for people to line up with one
another. In the five-person scene of Figure~\ref{fig:observability} no pedestrian
is occluded at all and the three missing ones are out of range, whereas the
twenty-person square scene has five occluded and ten out of range. Statements in
this paper about occlusion refer to limited-range sensing with inter-agent
occlusion, and the belief maintains previously observed tracks through the
resulting gaps; it does not place a prior on people who have never been seen.

The tested planner uses 16 prediction steps, 512 sampled sequences, four search
iterations, and an elite fraction of 0.08. A geometric human buffer of 0.10\,m is
included. These settings are extracted from the completed experimental configuration,
not from potentially different defaults in a later working tree.
Early experiments using 1.2\,m/s are not merged into the tables in this draft.

\subsubsection{Calibration Rather Than Policy Training}
The method does not undergo imitation or reinforcement learning.
It nevertheless contains parameters and calibrated alternatives; training-free
does not mean data-free or tuning-free.
For the fair calibration study, each noise condition has 100 five-person
development episodes, using case identifiers 3600--3699. Working-point selection
uses development cases 3650--3699. Reuse within this development pool is allowed
for model construction and selection, but its calibration coverage is not presented
as an independent final test.

Clean observations have zero injected measurement noise. Severe observations add
position standard deviation 0.10\,m, velocity standard deviation 0.20\,m/s, and
detection probability 0.80. Methods receive the same noise-condition information
and development trajectories. Testing a method configured for severe noise against
an alternative calibrated only on clean observations would answer a different
transfer question and is not the fair-noise comparison reported here.

Table~\ref{tab:interventions} defines the ablation arms. With the exception of the
explicit CV-memory control, the relevant uncertainty alternatives reuse the Bayesian
track set and predicted means. This controls the geometry presented to the planner
while varying its risk representation.

The CV-memory arm stores the last observation and extrapolates velocity with its
own one-second expiry and visible-empty-space management. It is not identical
to posterior mean. The posterior-mean intervention retains the Bayesian track
selection but removes both covariance and planning existence weights.
The existence-one intervention leaves existence-based track management intact.
Consequently, it isolates the planner's use of existence, not the entire role of
existence in the pipeline.

The age-margin control uses
\begin{equation}
m(a,k)=\kappa\big((a+k)\Delta t\big)^\eta.
\label{eq:agemargin}
\end{equation}
Its coefficients are fitted from development residuals. Five target-coverage
levels, 0.75, 0.85, 0.90, 0.95, and 0.975, define candidate working points.
The control removes covariance and existence inputs from the geometric planner
to avoid unintentionally retaining the full probability mechanism.
The fitted severe-noise margins are larger than the clean margins; this control
does respond to the known noise condition through calibration, although not to
online person-specific residuals.

Each family's working point is selected on development data using the registered
criterion: among points whose empirical collision rate is within two percentage
points of that family's minimum, choose the shortest failure-penalized time, with
the smaller point index breaking ties. This supplies an explicit selection rule,
but does not force all families to have the same true collision probability.
The resulting selected-point comparison is therefore not automatically a
matched-safety Pareto comparison. Small development samples also make point
selection uncertain; the final independent table must retain its frozen selection.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
\subsubsection{Outcome and Time Definitions}
Each episode is assigned one of three mutually exclusive audited outcomes.
A recorded collision takes precedence over a nominal goal event.
A success requires reaching the goal without an audited overlap. Remaining episodes
are failures to finish within the deadline.
The principal rates are success (SR), collision (CR), and timeout (TR).
The stored collision-union and success-without-overlap flags define the completed
tables. The independent final audit additionally reconciles those flags against
the raw swept-overlap fields and environment event.

To avoid measuring speed only on the episodes a method happens to complete, define
\begin{equation}
T_p^{(e)}=
\begin{cases}
T_{\mathrm{goal}}^{(e)},&\text{audited success},\\
T_{\max},&\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad
\bar T_p=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{e=1}^{n}T_p^{(e)}.
\label{eq:penaltytime}
\end{equation}
We call this \emph{failure-penalized task time}, not average arrival time.
It combines completion and speed: a lower value need not imply that successful
trajectories are individually faster.
Path length, mean progress, common-success paired arrival time, minimum clearance,
and actual overlap steps are complementary measures for the final evaluation.
An efficiency claim at equal safety requires safety non-inferiority or a
predefined risk-budget comparison, not merely overlapping collision confidence
intervals.

\subsubsection{Pairing, Uncertainty, and Repeated Layouts}
Methods are paired by scene and case identifier, with the same initial layout.
Macro rates give each of the six scenes equal weight. With 100 episodes per
scene, these equal the pooled rates. Binary comparisons use the exact paired
McNemar test, based on discordant episodes, rather than an unpaired Fisher test.
Time intervals use 4,000 paired bootstrap resamples within each scene.
The layout is retained as the comparison unit; individual control steps are not
independent navigation trials.

The historical 5000--5099 layouts have been inspected and reused in later
development. Their results remain useful as controlled descriptive evidence,
but must not be relabeled as a newly held-out confirmation.
The severe-noise cohort uses cases 5100--5699 and was independent of its own
calibration at execution. It too becomes historical evidence once inspected.
Reported historical $p$-values are exploratory and unadjusted. The final independent
comparison must apply its registered primary contrast and multiple-comparison
procedure, with no post-test changes to the selected working points.

Repeating planner seeds measures sensitivity to stochastic trajectory search.
It does not create independent training seeds or new layout samples.
Repeated runs of the same layout must remain clustered in any pooled uncertainty
estimate. A non-significant difference does not demonstrate equivalence, and a
small collision count can leave a practically important difference unresolved.

\subsection{External Baselines}
The completed historical full-observation benchmark includes ORCA, CADRL,
LSTM-RL, SARL, DSRNN, and AttnGraph. Retained checkpoints are evaluated in the
local harness. They are not retrained under an equal optimization budget, and
their original publication rates are not substituted for local outcomes.
An apparent large gap therefore includes controller design, training provenance,
and possible adaptation effects. The AttnGraph timeout pattern and the DSRNN
occlusion-mask contract require particular care.

These baselines share a limitation that this paper does not attempt to hide:
they are learned policies from an earlier generation, and beating them says
little about how the method compares with contemporary probabilistically
constrained MPC. A separate comparison against T-MPC++ and SH-MPC is under
construction on this benchmark and is deliberately not reported here, because
the interfacing work it requires is not finished. Two properties of that
comparison are already fixed and are stated now so that its eventual numbers
are read correctly. First, the comparators consume this project's detections,
track means and marginal predictive covariances rather than performing their own
inference, so any such table compares constraint formulations under a shared
posterior, not end-to-end systems. Second, the two families express risk
differently: the formulation here allocates a per-step budget that varies over
the horizon, whereas the ellipsoidal and scenario constraints of those methods
are configured with a single risk level applied at every step. Under a predictive
covariance that grows with the prediction interval these are not interchangeable
settings, and matching the nominal risk numbers would not make the two
conservatism levels equal.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} provides the central controlled comparison.
Discarding a pedestrian when it becomes unobservable results in 67.17\% success
and 32.83\% collision. Constant-velocity memory increases success to 93.00\%
and reduces collision to 7.00\%, showing that much of the difference from the
sensor-only controller is attributable to retaining information across
observation gaps.

The full posterior improves further, reaching 97.17\% success and 2.00\%
collision. Relative to CV memory, this is a 4.17 percentage-point increase
in success and a 5.00 percentage-point reduction in collision.
The collision count falls from 42 to 12, a 71.4\% relative reduction.
There are 37 paired episodes in which CV memory collides and the full method does
not, against seven in the opposite direction. The exact paired collision
$p$-value is $5.30\times10^{-6}$.
The paired bootstrap interval for the collision-rate difference is
$[-7.00,-2.83]$ percentage points.

This safety improvement should not be rewritten as an unconditional speed improvement.
Failure-penalized task time increases from 12.091\,s to 12.220\,s:
a paired difference of 0.129\,s, with a 95\% bootstrap interval of
$[-0.182,0.439]$\,s. The point estimate is approximately a 1.1\% time increase.
The full method has five timeouts where the CV-memory arm has none.
The evidence supports substantially fewer collisions with a small observed
time difference, not simultaneous statistically established improvement in
every metric.

\subsection{Position Covariance and Existence Weighting}
The posterior-mean arm achieves 93.50\% success and 6.50\% collision.
Because this arm retains the Bayesian track set and predicted means, its difference
from the full method is not explained simply by placing more remembered positions
in the planner. The full method reduces collisions from 39 to 12
($p=2.53\times10^{-5}$) and increases success by 3.67 percentage points
($p=0.00126$). Its penalized-time difference is $+0.191$\,s, with interval
$[-0.123,0.510]$\,s.

Retaining covariance while fixing all planning existence weights to one yields
96.67\% success and 2.67\% collision. This recovers most of the observed difference
between posterior mean and the full method. The remaining four-collision difference
between existence-one and full Bayes has exact paired $p=0.289$;
the 0.50 percentage-point success difference has $p=0.549$.
Thus, the current data most clearly support position-uncertainty-aware planning.
They do not establish a significant additional navigation benefit from existence
weighting.

This finding has two implications for the contribution statement. First, the
Bernoulli--Gaussian formulation is an accurate description of the implemented state,
but the empirical claim should not put all its emphasis on the Bernoulli component.
Second, setting a planning existence weight to one does not remove the filter's
existence-based track management. A claim that existence modeling is altogether
unnecessary would require a separate, properly matched management intervention.

Equation~\eqref{eq:agecov} makes age-dependent uncertainty a strong simple explanation
of the clean-condition result. Table~\ref{tab:age} tests a calibrated geometric
margin with this information. At the independently selected development points,
age margin attains 92.67\% success, 1.67\% collision, and 5.67\% timeout.
The full method attains 97.17\%, 2.00\%, and 0.83\%, respectively.

Age margin has two fewer collisions, a difference not resolved by the paired test
($p=0.804$). However, it has 27 fewer successes ($p=9.85\times10^{-5}$)
and 29 additional timeouts ($p=1.31\times10^{-7}$).
The full method reduces failure-penalized task time by 1.912\,s, with a paired
95\% interval of $[-2.207,-1.617]$\,s for full minus age margin.
In the dense-square scene, success is 92\% for the full method and 73\%
for age margin.

The selected geometric control is therefore more prone to freezing in dense
conditions. The result is consistent with an advantage from evaluating local
collision mass instead of treating the whole enlarged region as an obstacle.
It does not prove equal safety: absence of a significant collision difference
is not a safety-equivalence test. Nor does one fitted power-law margin establish
that every deterministic duration-aware construction must fail.
The supported statement is that this calibrated, budget-matched control does
not recover the full method's completion and penalized-time performance.

13.50 percentage points. This is a materially different operating pattern.

duration-dependent inflated region cannot express the same local probability
tradeoff as the other tested risk representations.

The most important inference from this experiment is negative but informative:
fair calibration substantially narrows the apparent Bayesian advantage.
EWMA and conformal remain viable alternatives. A previous comparison in which
one method used noise-aware settings and another retained clean calibration
would not isolate the uncertainty algorithm itself.

\subsection{Discussion and Limitations}
The strongest current conclusion is that a deterministic location is an insufficient
summary for the tested occluded-track planner. Full-posterior planning materially
reduces collision relative to both CV memory and posterior mean. The covariance
intervention accounts for most of that change. This result is more informative than
a standalone high success rate because it controls the downstream planner.

The next claim is narrower. The tested duration-aware geometric margin does not
match completion and penalized time, especially in dense square crossings.
This is evidence against that specific simple substitute, not against every
geometric risk formulation. Under severe noise, calibrated EWMA and conformal
controls remain competitive; the experiments do not establish that recursive
Bayesian covariance is universally necessary.

The evaluation is limited to the tested simulation dynamics, association assumptions,
and calibrated uncertainty families. The matched-tracker alternatives isolate risk
representation but do not compare entirely independent perception systems.
Performance against contemporary continuous planners and transfer to a physical
platform are outside the completed evaluation.

\section{CONCLUSION}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper has presented a training-free crowd-navigation framework that maintains
explicit beliefs over intermittently observed pedestrians and consumes those
beliefs in continuous trajectory optimization.
Its recursive state representation preserves occluded tracks, while collision-region
probability and route-separated search translate uncertainty into executable motion.
The formulation does not depend on a learned temporal backbone or a trained policy.

Completed six-scene experiments show a reduction from 42 to 12 collisions relative
to constant-velocity memory under the same MPC, and from 39 to 12 relative to
posterior-mean planning. These gains occur with a small observed increase in
failure-penalized task time. Position covariance supplies the clearest incremental
benefit; an independent advantage from planning existence weights remains unresolved.
A duration-dependent margin is more prone to freezing, but fairness-matched
severe-noise experiments also show that EWMA and conformal alternatives can
remain competitive.

The present evidence supports uncertainty-aware planning through occlusion, not
universal Bayesian superiority, guaranteed safety, or completed sim-to-real transfer.
